Dig into the API surface under /Desktop/test-repo/apps/backend, I want to know which endpoints have basically become dead weight without anyone noticing. The point is to pay down some tech debt. But I do not want us breaking prod to do it, so err on the cautious side the whole way through.

Treat this the way a backend engineer with a few scars would. An endpoint being quiet is not a reason to kill it. Some of our most important routes barely get called. So before you flag anything for retirement, go look at how it's actually used, what leans on it, and what falls over if it's gone. Take your time per endpoint. No shortcuts.

Here's what I actually want out of this:

A running inventory of every API we expose. A real sense of how each one gets used in prod, not how we assume it does. The ones that stopped pulling their weight. And a few safe, boring, low-drama removals that won't blow up somebody's integration.

Nothing becomes a removal candidate off a single signal. I want the traffic, the code deps, the docs, and the client usage all checked before that call gets made.

Mapping the surface : Start from the latest commit on the default branch. Then go find everything we expose. REST routes, GraphQL queries and mutations, webhooks, the public stuff, the internal stuff, and anything that's been versioned. For each one, grab the route and method, the controller, service, module, and repo path, whatever auth it needs, its version, and whether it's public or internal. If one logical endpoint has two or three versions live, don't lump them. Each version is its own record.

Production usage : The monitoring data sits in the "mock api data" Google Sheet, covering May 1 through June 29, 2026 (Asia/Kolkata). Pull total requests, unique clients, error rate, average latency, peak traffic, the last time it was hit, how requests were spread out, and the auth method. Throw out the fake traffic first. Health checks, monitoring probes, synthetic checks, load tests, uptime pings. None of that counts. I only care about real callers.

Lining code up against traffic : Now cross them. Some endpoints will fall into obvious groups: never called at all, basically zero prod requests, under 25 hits across the whole window, deprecated but somehow still getting traffic, internal-only, or old legacy versions. Whatever bucket something lands in, tell me why it landed there. And I'll say it again because it matters: volume alone doesn't decide the bucket. Dependencies, before you breathe a word about deleting anything.

Grep the whole codebase for references first. Front-end, mobile, internal services, background workers, cron jobs, event consumers, webhooks, SDKs, shared libs, public docs, OpenAPI and Swagger specs. All of it.

If something's actively using it, it stays. Full stop. And if you honestly can't tell whether a dependency exists, don't fill the gap with a guess. Kick it to manual review.

Who's still calling : Figure out the callers per endpoint. Web app, mobile, an internal service, a third party, a partner, or just unknown. Note how many clients, the last one you saw, the last request time, and their version if that's available. Can't ID all of them? Say so, plainly. I would rather have an honest gap than a confident fiction. 

Removal risk : Three buckets, and only three.
Safe to Remove is a high bar. Every one of these has to be true: no prod traffic, no code deps, no documented integrations, no known clients. Miss any one and it doesn't qualify. Needs Review is where most of the fuzzy stuff goes. Traffic is barely a trickle, the dependency picture has holes, some legacy integration might still be lurking, nobody's sure who owns it, or it just needs another pass. Keep is the easy one. Active traffic, an internal system depends on it, public clients rely on it, or it's part of a version we still support. Whichever bucket, show your reasoning. I want to follow the logic, not just see the verdict.

Tech debt score: Rough out the debt each endpoint carries. Think about its age, how much it's used, how gnarly the code is, what it costs to maintain, its security exposure, the doc overhead, and how many things depend on it. Score it Low, Medium, High, or Critical, and tell me how you got there.

Ground rules, in case any of the above gets ambiguous

Cleanup never wins over safety. Thin evidence means no delete recommendation, period. Keep what you verified separate from what you're guessing. When you're missing something, name exactly what's missing and mark it Needs Review. Don't invent anything — not how a thing works, not what it depends on, not who owns it, not where data lives, not when something happened. And any time your confidence is low, tell me why it's low.
